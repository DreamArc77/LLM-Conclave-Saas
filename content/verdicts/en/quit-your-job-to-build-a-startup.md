## Council verdict
The council unanimously recommends that you do not quit your job yet. You should preserve your primary income while actively de-risking the venture by validating customer demand, testing willingness to pay, and building an initial prototype. Quitting immediately is only justified if you have an extensive personal financial runway and your employment contract poses severe legal risks to building as a side venture. Ultimately, your resignation should be dictated by concrete traction and genuine operational necessity, not founder impatience. Overall confidence level: 8/10.

## Where the council agreed
* **Demand risk comes first:** Resigning prematurely burns personal runway on testing basic assumptions, which should ideally be validated before forfeiting a steady income.
* **Financial security protects decision-making:** A reliable paycheck prevents desperation, ensuring you do not compromise on pricing, take unfavorable equity terms, or pursue shortsighted pivots out of panic.
* **Objective departure triggers:** Resignation should be triggered by external validation—such as paying customers, committed investor funding, or market demand that outstrips your part-time capacity.

## Where it disagreed
* **Viability of moonlighting:** While building on nights and weekends was widely proposed, DeepSeek and Gemini cautioned that strict intellectual property assignment clauses and daytime B2B sales cycles often make side-project development legally perilous or practically impossible.
* **Runway benchmarks:** The council differed on whether a strict 12–18 month cash reserve is universally mandatory, with Luna and DeepSeek arguing that required savings should scale flexibly according to personal burn rate, dependents, and how quickly you could get rehired if the startup fails.

## Risks and caveats
* **Intellectual property exposure:** Developing your venture while employed may breach employment agreements or give your current employer ownership rights over your code, product, or ideas.
* **Execution fatigue versus market timing:** Prolonging the validation phase while working full-time risks severe founder burnout, while moving too slowly may close a time-sensitive market opportunity.

## The debate, round by round

### Round 1 — Independent answers

#### GPT-6 Luna

Don’t quit yet—unless you already have strong evidence people will pay for your product and enough savings to cover at least 6–12 months.

The idea may change once you test it with customers, so validate demand before giving up your income. A financial runway reduces pressure to make bad decisions. And if you can, build a prototype or get paying customers while still employed.

Quit when traction is real, the opportunity needs your full-time attention, and the downside is manageable. Confidence: 8/10 in this default recommendation; the right choice depends on your savings, obligations, and startup traction.

#### DeepSeek V4.1 Flash

Usually no — not yet. Keep the job while you validate, then quit once demand is proven.

Decisive reasons: (1) Runway buys honesty. A salary lets you test the idea without desperation distorting your decisions or forcing premature fundraising. (2) Most startup risk is demand risk, not effort risk; quitting adds financial pressure without reducing it. (3) The strongest signal to quit is paying customers or committed users — evidence you can't get from a plan.

Quit now only if: you have 12+ months of expenses saved, a cofounder, early traction or a hard market deadline, and no dependents relying on your income. Otherwise, cut to part-time or build nights and weekends, set a 3-6 month validation deadline, and quit when the data — not the itch — says go.

Confidence: 7/10.

#### Gemini 3.8 Flash

My recommendation is no—do not quit yet. Transition only after you achieve clear customer validation or secure funding. 

The decisive reasons are:

1. Unvalidated assumptions burn cash: Quitting early drains your financial runway on ideas rather than execution. You must validate real demand and build a prototype while still drawing a paycheck.
2. Financial stress corrupts strategy: Quitting without 12–18 months of personal living expenses forces desperate, short-term decisions regarding product direction, pricing, and equity.
3. The side-project filter works: Building nights and weekends tests both the viability of the concept and your genuine stamina for the grueling reality of founder life without existential risk.

If you already have paying customers or committed investor capital, quit immediately. Otherwise, de-risk first.

Confidence: 8/10.

### Round 2 — Cross-examination

#### GPT-6 Luna

I agree with the default: don’t quit before testing demand, because a salary lets you learn without financial pressure. I’d build a prototype, talk to customers, and seek evidence they’ll pay while employed if feasible.

I disagree with treating 12–18 months of savings, funding, or paying customers as universal requirements. The right runway depends on your obligations and burn rate, and some opportunities need full-time focus or have genuine deadlines. Consider quitting when the evidence, opportunity cost of staying, and personal finances together make the risk acceptable—not just because the idea feels compelling.

#### DeepSeek V4.1 Flash

I agree with all three: don't quit on an unvalidated idea, and runway genuinely buys honest decisions. My one disagreement is with the rigid thresholds—"12–18 months" and "nights and weekends" are defaults, not rules. Some businesses can't be tested part-time (network effects, market timing, sales-heavy B2B), and moonlighting can breach IP or non-compete clauses, turning a side project into a legal problem. What matters isn't a savings number but your downside floor: dependents, debt, health insurance, and whether you could get rehired within months. If that floor is safe and the opportunity is time-sensitive, quitting early can be correct. Validate first; don't worship the calendar.

#### Gemini 3.8 Flash

I agree with the council’s consensus: default to **no** until you have validated demand and at least 6–12 months of runway. Financial desperation forces flawed product decisions and bad equity compromises.

However, I disagree with their blanket assumption that you should always build while employed. If your employment contract has strict intellectual property assignment clauses, moonlighting risks your employer owning your venture. Furthermore, complex B2B sales and regulated markets require daytime customer access that nights and weekends cannot support.

Only quit immediately if employment agreements create severe legal IP risks, you have substantial personal runway, and speed is strictly existential. Otherwise, preserve your cash flow and prove willingness to pay before resigning.
