## Council verdict
Startups should treat B2B as the safer default for early survival, but only if they can reach decision-makers and solve a high-value operational pain point. B2B accelerates runway preservation because a handful of contracts validate willingness to pay, whereas B2C demands massive scale to monetize low average revenue per user. However, this default must be overridden if founders possess an unfair consumer distribution channel, a viral product mechanic, or lack the industry network required for enterprise sales. Overall council confidence: 8/10.

## Where the council agreed
* **Survivability and unit economics:** B2B allows startups to reach meaningful cash flow and runway extension with dozens of clients, whereas B2C requires immense scale to achieve sustainability.
* **Validation efficiency:** B2B discovery relies on direct, low-cost founder outreach and explicit ROI discussions, avoiding the expensive paid user acquisition that often drains early B2C budgets.
* **Distribution over market size:** The decision must be dictated by who the founding team can reach repeatedly and cheaply, rather than total addressable market projections.
* **Prerequisites for B2C:** B2C should only be pursued first if the product features native network effects, low marginal delivery costs, or an existing organic audience.

## Where it disagreed
* **Strength of the B2B default:** Gemini and DeepSeek initially advocated for an explicit B2B default due to cash-flow discipline, whereas Luna warned that treating B2B as a blanket starting point ignores the lethal risks of complex buying committees, budget access barriers, and protracted enterprise sales cycles.

## Risks and caveats
* **Pilot purgatory and custom feature traps:** Choosing B2B without clear procurement access can stall a startup in unpaid, year-long proof-of-concept trials or force the team into becoming an outsourced services agency.
* **Premature consumer acquisition burn:** Choosing B2C without an inherent organic acquisition engine often leads founders to burn scarce capital on performance marketing before validating product-market fit or user retention.

## The debate, round by round

### Round 1 — Independent answers

#### GPT-6 Luna

Default to B2B if you have a specific, costly business problem and can reach the buyer; choose B2C if you have a compelling consumer need and a credible path to low-cost, repeatable distribution.

B2B often wins early because customer value is easier to quantify, a few customers can generate meaningful revenue, and you can validate willingness to pay directly. B2C can scale much larger, but typically demands stronger product-led growth, brand, or network effects—and can be expensive to acquire users.

Decide based on your team’s insight and access, not market size alone. Confidence: 8/10.

#### DeepSeek V4.1 Flash

Default to B2B first for most new startups. Businesses buy to solve costly problems, so you get revenue, clear ROI, and direct feedback before scaling. CAC is usually lower and distribution can be founder-led sales; you don't need millions of users to validate. B2C often needs viral loops, cheap capital, and strong retention, which are hard pre-product/market fit. Choose B2C first only if the product has inherent network effects, low marginal cost, and a credible organic acquisition path. Otherwise, B2B's faster learning and cash discipline win. Confidence: 7/10.

#### Gemini 3.8 Flash

Default to B2B first, unless you possess proprietary viral distribution or massive upfront capital.

Decisive reasons:
1. Immediate monetization and higher contract value: B2B buyers pay upfront for clear ROI or operational relief. You only need a dozen paying clients to achieve meaningful runway, whereas B2C requires immense scale to monetize via subscriptions or ads.
2. Low-cost customer discovery: Early B2B validation relies on direct, unscalable founder outreach (cold email, network introductions, LinkedIn). In contrast, testing B2C usually burns scarce cash on paid acquisition before product-market fit is established.
3. Survivability: B2B shortens the time to cash-flow neutrality, shielding early-stage founders from brutal consumer churn and fickle venture capital markets.

Confidence: 9/10

### Round 2 — Cross-examination

#### GPT-6 Luna

I agree with the others that B2B is often a strong starting point when the problem is costly, the buyer is reachable, and value is measurable. A few paid customers can teach you a lot.

I disagree with making B2B the default for most startups. B2B sales can be slow and expensive, and “clear ROI” does not guarantee access to budget or a short buying process. B2C can be the better first market when the team has a distribution advantage, strong user pull, or a product that spreads naturally. Choose the market where you can reach users, test willingness to pay, and learn fastest—not by customer type alone.

#### DeepSeek V4.1 Flash

I largely agree with the B2B default, but the framing is too binary. B2B wins when the problem is expensive, the buyer is reachable, and sales cycles are short — otherwise you get a year of pilots and no revenue, which is worse than B2C. I disagree with treating B2C as inherently capital-dependent: a genuine network effect or shareable artifact can validate for near zero. Decide on distribution, not customer type: who can you reach repeatedly and cheaply, and who feels the pain weekly? If both are equal, pick B2B — faster cash, sharper feedback. Confidence: 7/10.

#### Gemini 3.8 Flash

I agree with the council that B2B is the safer default for early survival. It offers faster cash flow, clearer ROI, and direct customer feedback without needing millions of users. 

However, I disagree with treating B2B as an automatic choice regardless of founder-market fit. Forcing consumer-oriented builders into B2B often results in stalled pilots, endless procurement hurdles, and crippling custom-feature requests. 

The decision should hinge on your team’s unfair distribution advantage: choose B2B if you have industry access and can execute founder-led sales. Choose B2C only if you already control an organic acquisition channel—like a built-in audience or proven viral mechanic—and have the capital to endure slow, low-ARPU monetization. Confidence: 9/10.
