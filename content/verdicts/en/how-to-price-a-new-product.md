## Council verdict
Price novel products using value-based pricing anchored to the economic cost of the customer's status quo—such as existing workarounds, manual labor, or lost revenue—rather than cost-plus guesswork. Establish an absolute sustainability floor using your unit economics, then target initial pricing toward the higher, defensible end of the quantified value created. Validate willingness to pay through live purchasing commitments, such as cohort tests or pilot contracts, rather than relying strictly on stated survey intent. Adjust iteratively based on conversion, retention, and margin. Overall confidence: 8/10.

## Where the council agreed
* **The status quo is the competitor:** Even without direct market alternatives, the true pricing anchor is the quantified cost of the customer's current inefficiencies, workarounds, or missed revenue.
* **Bounded economic boundaries:** Unit economics set the non-negotiable floor required for business viability, while the customer’s economic value created (EVC) defines the rational ceiling.
* **Behavior over stated intent:** Live financial commitments (pilot contracts, pre-orders, cohort conversion, and retention) provide far more reliable signal than abstract willingness-to-pay surveys.
* **Iterative refinement:** Initial pricing should be treated as an empirical hypothesis tested across customer segments or tiers, adjusting as empirical usage and retention data emerge.

## Where it disagreed
* **Initial pricing posture (Skimming vs. Moderate):** Gemini strongly advocates price skimming to protect long-term pricing power and signal category-defining value, whereas Luna cautions that starting near the top of the range risks suppressing early adoption and customer feedback when value realization is uncertain.
* **Introductory discounting and pilots:** Members diverged on execution mechanisms; some favored structured introductory discounts or tiered pilot pricing to accelerate discovery, while others argued that discounting contaminates willingness-to-pay signals and attracts deal-seekers.

## Risks and caveats
* **Value perception gaps:** Quantified economic value calculated on paper often fails to account for customer implementation friction, switching costs, and delayed time-to-value.
* **Asymmetric price mobility:** It is easy to cut prices or introduce lower tiers later, but raising prices on an underpriced novel product is notoriously difficult and risks severe customer backlash.

## The debate, round by round

### Round 1 — Independent answers

#### GPT-6 Luna

Use a value-based launch price, bounded by unit economics: set a viable floor from costs, then estimate willingness to pay through customer interviews, concept tests, and small live-market experiments. Start with a testable price and adjust based on conversion, retention, and margin—not stated interest alone. If uncertainty is high, test a few prices across comparable customer groups rather than changing the price for everyone.

This is preferable because costs establish sustainability, customers’ perceived value determines demand, and real purchase behavior reduces guesswork when comparable products are absent.

Confidence: 8/10.

#### DeepSeek V4.1 Flash

Price on value, not cost or guesswork: estimate willingness-to-pay via Van Westendorp, conjoint, or customer interviews, then launch with 2–3 tiers and a premium anchor. Set initial price near the top of the defensible value range, with a time-limited intro discount or pilot pricing to learn. Iterate using A/B tests, win/loss data, and sales objections. Decisive reasons: 1) Without comparables, cost-plus and competitor anchors systematically misprice; 2) value-based tiers segment heterogeneous WTP and capture surplus; 3) live experimentation turns uncertainty into data before you lock in. Confidence: 8/10.

#### Gemini 3.8 Flash

Use value-based pricing anchored to the economic cost of the customer's status quo, implemented via a price-skimming strategy. Even without direct competitors, customers currently solve the problem through manual labor, workarounds, or lost revenue. Quantify that pain to set your baseline.

Decisive reasons:
1. **The status quo is the competitor:** Pricing against the quantified economic value created (EVC)—money saved or generated—creates an objective anchor when market comparables do not exist.
2. **Preserves pricing power:** It is easy to discount, add lower tiers, or reduce prices as you scale, but nearly impossible to raise an artificially low introductory price.
3. **Value signaling:** For novel innovations, a premium price psychologically signals category-defining efficacy and attracts high-conviction early adopters willing to provide critical feedback.

Confidence: 8/10.

### Round 2 — Cross-examination

#### GPT-6 Luna

I agree that customer value—not cost alone—should guide pricing, and that the status quo (manual work, workarounds, or lost revenue) can provide a useful value anchor. I also agree that real purchase behavior is more reliable than stated willingness to pay.

I’d keep unit economics as a viability floor, though, and I wouldn’t default to pricing near the top of an estimated value range or using price skimming. Those approaches can suppress adoption when value is uncertain or hard for buyers to realize. Start with a defensible price hypothesis, then test different prices with comparable customer groups and track conversion, retention, and margin. Use interviews and surveys to shape the tests, not as proof of what customers will pay.

#### DeepSeek V4.1 Flash

Agree with Gemini's core: without comparables, the customer's status quo is the only objective anchor. Quantify the cost of the workaround or lost revenue, then price at a defensible fraction of that value created. Luna's floor-from-cost and cohort-split testing are right too.

I disagree with DeepSeek's default of a premium anchor plus time-limited intro discount. Discounts attract deal-seekers, train customers to wait, and contaminate the willingness-to-pay signal you're trying to read. Launch with 2–3 tiers at full price, segment testers by cohort, and let tier mix—not discounts—reveal demand. Cut price only if conversion and retention both stay weak.

#### Gemini 3.8 Flash

I agree with Gemini that "no comparables" is a fallacy: the baseline is always the customer’s status quo—manual workarounds, inefficiencies, or lost revenue. Quantifying this Economic Value to the Customer (EVC) sets the rational ceiling, while unit economics (GPT-6) set the absolute floor. 

I agree with starting high via price skimming, as lowering price or discounting later preserves pricing power, whereas raising it destroys goodwill. 

However, I disagree with DeepSeek’s reliance on hypothetical surveys like Van Westendorp; customers cannot accurately price unprecedented products in abstract surveys. Price against the quantified value of replacing their current workaround, launch high with pilot contracts or pre-orders to test actual purchasing behavior, and iterate downward if necessary.
